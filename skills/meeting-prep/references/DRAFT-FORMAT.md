# Draft format spec

The shape every outbound draft takes. A draft is something the reader
sends before the meeting, written so it can be sent as is. Nothing sends on its own: every
draft lands in the approval queue, and a person approves, edits or
rejects it.

## When to draft

Only for **asking or telling someone something before the meeting** —
usually what makes a move in the room possible (the number you need in
hand, the person you need briefed). If the move itself happens in the
room, it stays in the brief.

**When the meeting expects something from the reader** — a pre-read, a
short deck, a written proposal — draft that too, as its own file in the
drafts folder, following the brief's position. It is a deliverable, not a
message: the 120-word limit doesn't apply, the accuracy rules do.

Never draft a follow-up to a meeting that hasn't happened. A draft may
not say what was decided, agreed or said in a meeting that is still
ahead of its date.

## The header

```
Type: draft
Date: <the day it would be sent>
From: <the brief's reader>
To: <one person — or a small group with the same ask>
Subject: <what this is about, in under ten words — the recipient decides from this alone whether to open it now>
Channel: <email | chat message | text | note>
Supports: <the How step this makes possible — its number and a plain paraphrase, no quotation marks — or "logistics" for a dial-in, a room, a pre-read deadline>
```

## The body

- **A short greeting, then the ask.** "Hey <first name>," on its own
  line is fine. Straight after it: what you need, from whom, by when.
  No context before the ask.
- **Logistics on one line.** When the ask is about a meeting: what,
  where, and the time as a range — *"budget review, Room 4B,
  14:00–14:30, Tue 3 Mar"*.
- **One subject per draft; related asks as bullets.** Things the same
  person must do for the same meeting go in one draft, as a short
  bulleted list. Asks about different subjects are different drafts.
- **No pronoun that could point two ways.** "His name" beside two men is
  a guess; name the role instead ("the source's name").
- **120 words or fewer.**
- **A deadline, named as a date and time.** Never "today", "tomorrow" or
  "this morning" — the draft may be read on a different day from the one
  it was written.
- **One line of why.** Enough for the recipient to act without calling
  back. Not the brief's whole argument.
- **Written in the sender's voice, to the recipient's position.** A note
  to a manager and a note to a peer are different notes.

## Accuracy — the same rules as a brief

A draft reports. It carries a real person's name into someone else's
inbox, so it is held to the brief's accuracy rules without exception:

- Quotation marks mean verbatim from a source.
- Every figure traces to a source. A figure you don't have is the ask,
  not a guess.
- Cite the source file in a `Sources:` line at the end, below the body.
  The recipient never sees it; the fact checker needs it.

## Discretion — write for everyone who could see it

A message is read by more people than it is addressed to. A note is left
on a desk; an email is forwarded, read over a shoulder, kept for years;
a chat thread gets new members. Write for all of them.

- **Name nothing that would harm someone if the wrong person read it.**
  A source, a private promise, a dispute, a health or money matter. If
  the ask needs it, the ask happens in person: the draft says *"let's
  talk before 12:00"* and names no specifics.
- **Two facts that are harmless apart can be harmful together.** "The
  supplier" plus "their night-shift supervisor" plus "the delivery logs"
  names a source as surely as a name does. Read the draft as a stranger would.
- **When in doubt, don't draft.** Put it in the queue note: *too
  sensitive for writing — raise in person*, and say with whom.

## What a good draft is

> **Would you send it with one edit or fewer?**

A draft that needs rewriting is worse than no draft: it costs the same
time and adds a decision.

## When not to draft

- The message would be delicate enough that its wording **is** the
  decision — tell the reader in the brief instead, and let them write it.
- The ask depends on the meeting's outcome.
- The only purpose is to look busy.

Say so in a line at the top of the queue instead of forcing a draft.
